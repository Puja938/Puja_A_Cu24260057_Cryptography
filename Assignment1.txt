Q1. Define cryptography in your own words. What are the three main goals it tries to achieve?
Answer: Cryptography is the science of protecting information by converting it into a form that unauthorized people cannot understand. Its three main goals are:
Confidentiality: Only authorized people can access information.
Integrity: Information should not be changed or modified without authorization.
Availability: Authorized users should be able to access information when required.
Q2. Differentiate between plaintext and ciphertext with a simple example.
Answer:

Ciphertext: The unreadable or encoded form of plaintext after encryption.
Example:
Plaintext: HELLO
After encryption using a Caesar cipher with a shift of 3:
Ciphertext: KHOOR

Q4. What is the difference between encryption and decryption?
Answer:
Encryption: It is the process of converting plaintext into ciphertext using an encryption algorithm and a key.
Decryption: It is the reverse process of converting ciphertext back into the original plaintext using the appropriate key.
Example:
Encryption: HELLO → KHOOR
Decryption: KHOOR → HELLO
Q5. Describe the two major types of cryptography – Symmetric Key Cryptography and Asymmetric Key Cryptography. Compare them based on key usage, speed, security, and typical use cases. Support your answer with examples of algorithms under each type.
Answer:
Cryptography is mainly divided into two types: symmetric-key cryptography and asymmetric-key cryptography.
1. Symmetric-Key Cryptography
Symmetric cryptography uses the same secret key for encryption and decryption. The communicating parties must securely share the secret key.
Working:
Plaintext → Encryption (Secret Key) → Ciphertext → Decryption (Same Secret Key) → Plaintext
Examples: AES, DES, 3DES and ChaCha20.
Advantages:
Generally fast and efficient.
Suitable for encrypting large amounts of data.
Requires relatively less computational power.
Disadvantages:
The secret key must be securely shared.
Key distribution becomes difficult when many parties communicate.
Uses: Bulk data encryption, file protection and secure data transfer.
. Asymmetric-Key Cryptography
Asymmetric cryptography, also called public-key cryptography, uses two related keys:
Public Key: Can be shared with others.
Private Key: Must be kept secret.
For confidential communication, the sender can encrypt information using the receiver's public key, and the receiver decrypts it using the corresponding private key.
Examples: RSA, ECC and ElGamal.
Advantages:
Makes secure key establishment easier.
Supports authentication and digital signatures.
The private key does not need to be shared with other parties.
Disadvantages:
Generally slower than symmetric cryptography.
Requires more computational resources.
Uses: Secure key exchange, digital signatures and authentication.
Feature Symmetric Cryptography Asymmetric Cryptography
Keys One shared secret key Public and private key pair
Encryption Secret key Typically public key for confidentiality
Decryption Same secret key Corresponding private key
Speed Generally faster Generally slower
Key distribution More difficult Public keys can be shared
Large data Very suitable Generally not used directly for bulk data
Examples AES, DES, 3DES RSA, ECC
Main use Data encryption Key establishment and digital signatures
Conclusion: Symmetric cryptography is efficient for encrypting large amounts of data, whereas asymmetric cryptography is useful for key establishment, authentication and digital signatures. Modern systems often combine both techniques.
Q6. What is hashing, and how is it different from encryption?
Answer:
Hashing is the process of converting data into a fixed-length value called a hash or message digest. It is generally a one-way process and is useful for detecting data modification.
Difference:
Hashing: Generally one-way; the original data is not intended to be recovered from the hash.
Encryption: Reversible using the appropriate decryption key.
Examples: SHA-256 and SHA-3 are hashing algorithms, while AES is an encryption algorithm.

Achieving confidentiality, integrity and non-repudiation together is challenging because each requires different security mechanisms. Encryption protects confidentiality, while cryptographic hashes and digital signatures help protect integrity and provide evidence of the sender's action.

Therefore, a real-world communication system must combine suitable cryptographic techniques with secure key management and authentication procedures

Q10. Critically discuss the evolution of cryptography from classical ciphers to modern cryptographic systems, evaluating the impact of computational advances on cryptographic security.
Answer:
Introduction
Cryptography has evolved from simple manual techniques to sophisticated mathematical and computer-based systems. This development has improved data protection while also creating new challenges for cryptographic security.
Caesar Cipher: Each letter is shifted by a fixed number of positions.
Vigenère Cipher: Uses a keyword to determine different shifts for different letters.
Rail Fence Cipher: Rearranges characters according to a transposition pattern.
These methods were simple to implement but had weaknesses. Frequency analysis and other cryptanalytic techniques could help attackers recover messages.
2. Cryptography During World War II
Cryptography became extremely important during World War II. The German Enigma machine was used to encrypt military communications. Allied cryptanalysts worked to break encrypted communications, and people such as Alan Turing played an important role in wartime codebreaking.
This period demonstrated the strategic importance of cryptography and cryptanalysis.
Development of Modern Cryptography
With the growth of computers, cryptography became increasingly mathematical and computational.
DES: Developed as a major encryption standard in the 1970s.
RSA: Introduced public-key cryptography in 1977.
AES: Became a widely used modern symmetric encryption standard.
ECC: Provided public-key cryptography based on elliptic curves.
Modern cryptography supports secure communication, digital payments, online banking and data protection.
. Impact of Computational Advances
Positive impacts:
Computers enable strong encryption to be implemented efficiently.
Modern cryptographic algorithms can protect large amounts of digital data.
Cryptographic protocols support secure communication over the Internet.
Challenges:
Faster computers can test more possible keys, making weak encryption easier to break.
Classical ciphers can be analyzed rapidly using computational methods.
Poor key management and implementation can weaken otherwise strong algorithms.
Advances in computing, including potential quantum computing capabilities, may threaten some existing public-key algorithms.
5. Critical Evaluation
The evolution of cryptography shows that security cannot depend only on keeping an algorithm secret or using a complicated method. Algorithms must be designed to resist known attacks, keys must be sufficiently strong, and systems must be updated as technology advances.
Classical ciphers are valuable for learning basic concepts, but modern digital systems require stronger, carefully evaluated cryptographic algorithms.
Conclusion
Cryptography has progressed from manual ciphers to advanced mathematical systems. Computational advances have improved the speed and usefulness of cryptography while also increasing attackers' capabilities. Therefore, secure systems require modern algorithms, appropriate key management and regular security updates.
Q11. Trace the historical development of cryptographic techniques, starting from classical methods through to modern computer-based cryptography. Highlight key turning points such as World War II cryptography and the invention of public-key cryptography.
Answer:
. Ancient Cryptography
One of the earliest known examples is the Caesar Cipher, associated with Julius Caesar. It uses a substitution technique in which letters are shifted by a fixed number of positions.
Example: With a shift of 3:
Plaintext: HELLO
Ciphertext: KHOOR
It was simple but not secure for modern applications.
2. Classical Cryptography
Later, more complex substitution and transposition techniques were developed.
Examples include:
Monoalphabetic substitution cipher
Vigenère cipher
Playfair cipher
Rail Fence cipher
Columnar transposition
These techniques attempted to make encrypted messages harder to analyze. However, many classical ciphers were vulnerable to cryptanalysis.
3. Cryptography During World War II
During World War II, cryptography became crucial for protecting military communications. The German Enigma machine was used to encrypt messages.
Allied cryptanalysts worked to break these encrypted communications. Alan Turing and other codebreakers contributed to wartime cryptanalysis, demonstrating the importance of mathematics and systematic analysis in cryptography.
Development of Computer-Based Cryptography
With the development of computers, cryptography became more mathematical and computational.
DES: Developed as a major encryption standard in the 1970s.
RSA: Introduced public-key cryptography in 1977.
AES: Became a widely used modern symmetric encryption standard.
ECC: Introduced public-key cryptography based on elliptic curves.
These developments enabled stronger and more practical protection of digital information.
5. Invention of Public-Key Cryptography
Public-key cryptography introduced a system using two related keys: a public key that can be shared and a private key that must remain secret.
RSA became a major example of this approach. It made secure key establishment and digital signatures possible without requiring every communicating party to share the same secret key beforehand.
Modern Cryptography
Today, cryptography is widely used in:
HTTPS websites
Online banking
Digital payments
Messaging applications
Virtual Private Networks (VPNs)
Cloud computing
Digital signatures
Password protection
Wireless networks and digital certificates
Modern systems often combine asymmetric cryptography for key establishment with symmetric cryptography for efficient data encryption.
Conclusion
Cryptography has evolved from simple substitution and transposition ciphers to advanced computer-based systems. World War II highlighted the importance of cryptanalysis, while public-key cryptography introduced a new approach to key management and digital signatures. Today, cryptography is essential for protecting information in digital systems.
Q12. Who introduced the concept of public-key cryptography, and in which year?
Answer: Public-key cryptography was publicly introduced by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976. RSA, a well-known public-key cryptographic algorithm, was introduced by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman in 1977.
